Golden hour, decoded

The stretch of time right after sunrise and right before sunset when the sun sits low (below 6° elevation) and its light travels through more atmosphere — which scatters the blue and leaves warm, soft, flattering gold. It's why every photographer, filmmaker and balloon artist fights for it. It's not literally an hour — most evenings it's 35–50 minutes.
The page runs the same NOAA solar model used by pro photographers and astronomy apps: true solar position with refraction correction, plus your city's real time zone and daylight saving. Expect ±2 minutes — more than enough to schedule a party (and far more accurate than "sometime around sunset").
The earth is tilted 23.5° and racing around the sun. Sunset shifts ~1–2 minutes per day — later after the winter solstice, earlier after the summer one. That's why the sunset you "remember" from last year is wrong by 20 minutes, and why Isabella checks the real numbers for every single event date.
Yes — it's her notebook rule. Before every event she looks up the sunset time, then places the arch, the balloon drop and the photo moments to catch the light. Golden hour is the difference between party photos people post and party photos people print. (Her secret #10, straight from the Party Day Timeline.)
Clouds don't cancel golden hour — they soften it into a glowing, diffused version (often even more flattering). Indoors, golden hour means low warm light pouring through west-facing windows — so put the dessert table and the photo wall on the west side for the last hour of the day. The window still tells you when to aim for.
