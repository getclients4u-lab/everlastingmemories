Surprise Party Intel, Answered

Because the number-one way surprises die is the target asking "so what are we doing?" and someone — usually the planner — panicking and telling the truth. A codename keeps group texts vague ("Operation prep meeting, 5pm") and a cover story gives everyone one boring, agreed answer. Isabella's rule from 50+ surprise reveals: the decoy isn't optional, it's the whole plan.
The 10-point checklist is distilled from her real playbook: decor delivered in unmarked bags, inflated off-site so the house stays clean, guests parked down the street, group chat renamed to something dull, and a lookout with code words. Roughly one in four parties she decorates is a surprise reveal — she's had balloons hidden in trunks, closets and once, memorably, in a neighbor's garage.
That's the quiet-quote part: tell Isabella the reveal time, and she schedules the install for when the target is guaranteed out — the decoy text gets them out of the house, the install team is in and out in under two hours, and the garage door stays closed until the big moment. Timing is coordinated with your lookout signals, not a minute sooner.
You've got the code words for that: "code red" goes out on the group chat, everyone goes silent, lights off, and the target walks into a very calm, very empty living room. It's the oldest trick in the book — the more normal the house looks, the less they suspect. (This is also why Isabella never installs more than a few hours ahead.)
The party! That's when the rest of the toolbox takes over: run the Party Day Timeline so the night has a rhythm, cue the Party Playlist for the entrance, catch the Golden Hour Finder for the photo moment, and send guests to the Party Photo Hunt so every angle gets captured. The surprise is the opening act — the memory is the show.
